Your First Safari: Tips and Etiquette for the Bush

The Wildara Team27 August 20266 min read

A first safari is a little like learning a new language. At first you see only the obvious: the elephant, the lion, the giraffe on the skyline. Then, slowly, the details begin to speak. Fresh tracks in the dust, a tail flicking in the long grass, a sudden silence among the birds. Knowing how a safari day works, and how to behave around wildlife and people, helps you relax into that rhythm sooner. These are the tips we share with every guest heading into the bush for the first time.

The rhythm of a safari day

Safari days begin early. Animals are most active in the cool of the morning, and the soft light at sunrise is beautiful for photography, so many drives start around dawn with coffee before you leave. The middle of the day is often quieter, when animals rest in the shade and guests enjoy a picnic lunch in the park or a rest at the lodge. As the heat fades in the afternoon, wildlife becomes active again, and the golden hour before sunset is one of the most magical times of day. National park rules generally require vehicles to leave game-viewing areas by dusk, so evenings are spent around the fire or over dinner, swapping stories of the day.

In the vehicle: game drive etiquette

Your Land Cruiser is your window on the wilderness, and a few simple habits make every sighting better for you and for the animals. None of this is complicated, and most of it comes naturally once you have spent a morning in the bush with an experienced guide beside you. Good manners in the vehicle simply mean better sightings for everyone.

  • Stay inside the vehicle unless your guide says it is safe to step out
  • Keep voices low at sightings; animals are sensitive to noise
  • Avoid sudden movements and never stand on the seats
  • Do not feed or call to animals, even to attract their attention
  • Ask your guide before using flash, and avoid it entirely at night
  • Share the best views with others in the vehicle by taking turns
  • Keep all litter in the vehicle until you can dispose of it properly

Above all, trust your guide. Their positioning, timing and judgement come from years of experience in these parks. They read animal behaviour, know the park rules and understand when to wait and when to move on. If you have a particular wish, such as a leopard or a certain bird, simply tell them, and they will do their best to find it for you.

Respecting wildlife

The animals you see are wild, and our presence should never change their behaviour. A good guide keeps a respectful distance, switches off the engine when appropriate and gives animals space to move, hunt or rest. If a cheetah is stalking prey or a mother is with young cubs, the best sightings come from patience rather than closeness. Off-road driving is restricted in many areas of Tanzania's national parks to protect fragile habitats, and park rules must always be followed. Sometimes this means watching from a little further away than you might wish. In return, you help ensure that these landscapes remain wild and that animals stay relaxed around vehicles for future visitors.

Photography and people

Tanzania's people are among the warmest you will meet, and a smile and a greeting in Swahili go a long way. Jambo means hello and asante means thank you. When it comes to photography, however, the rule is simple: always ask before photographing anyone. Many people are happy to be photographed, but some are not, and their wishes should be respected. In some communities, people may ask for a small payment in return for a photograph. Your guide can explain local customs and help with introductions. Photographing government buildings, airports, police and military personnel is generally not permitted, so check with your guide if you are unsure.

Visiting communities with care

Cultural visits can be among the most rewarding parts of a safari, whether you meet a Maasai community, learn about the traditional life of the Hadzabe and Datoga around Lake Eyasi, or explore the markets of Arusha. These are living communities, not attractions, and visiting with humility and curiosity makes the experience richer for everyone. A few thoughtful habits help.

  • Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees, particularly in villages and towns
  • Ask questions and listen; your guide can help translate
  • Avoid handing out sweets or gifts to children, which can encourage begging
  • If you wish to contribute, ask about supporting local schools or projects
  • Buy crafts directly where possible, so income stays in the community

Patience and the art of looking

The best safari moments often reward those who wait. Sitting quietly at a waterhole as elephants arrive, or watching a pride of lions slowly wake in the late afternoon, can be more memorable than ticking off a long list of species. Ask your guide questions, learn to read the landscape and enjoy the smaller creatures too: the dung beetles, the lilac-breasted rollers, the dwarf mongooses peering from a termite mound. Discovery begins in the detail, and the more you look, the more you will see.

Frequently asked questions

What time do game drives start?

Most game drives start early, often around sunrise, because animals are most active in the cool morning hours. Many days include a full day in the park with a picnic lunch, while others feature a morning and an afternoon drive with a rest at midday. As your safari is private, your guide can adjust timings to suit your energy and interests.

Can I get out of the vehicle on a game drive?

For your safety, you should stay in the vehicle in wildlife areas unless your guide says it is safe to get out. There are designated picnic sites, viewpoints and rest areas where stepping out is permitted. If you would like to explore on foot, ask us about an optional walking safari with an experienced armed ranger.

Is it rude to photograph local people?

Photographing people without permission can feel intrusive, so always ask first. Many people are happy to agree, while others may prefer not to, or may ask for a small fee, which is a local custom in some areas. Your guide can help with introductions and translation, which often turns a quick photograph into a genuine conversation.

Should I tip my guide?

Tipping is customary on safari and is greatly appreciated, though it is always at your discretion. It is usually given at the end of the trip, in an envelope, directly to your guide. Lodges typically have a communal tip box for staff. Our article on safari costs includes typical ranges, offered as guidance only, to help you plan.
