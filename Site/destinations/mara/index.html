Northern Serengeti and the Mara River

Where the migration gathers on the riverbank and every crossing is a test of nerve.

Overview

The northern Serengeti feels different from the central plains. The land rises into rolling hills and wooded valleys, granite outcrops catch the evening light, and the Mara River cuts a deep, muddy channel along the border with Kenya. For much of the year it is one of the quietest corners of the park. Then, usually from July, the migration arrives, and the riverbanks become the setting for one of the most dramatic wildlife events on earth.

A crossing begins long before the first animal jumps. Herds of wildebeest and zebra build up on the bank, hesitating, pressing forward and pulling back, sometimes for hours. Then one animal commits and the rest follow in a surge of dust, noise and spray. Nile crocodiles wait in the water and lions and hyenas watch from the far side. It is raw, unpredictable and impossible to schedule, which is exactly why it moves people so deeply.

Watching well is an art. Our guides know the main crossing points around Kogatende and read the herds' behaviour, positioning the vehicle at a respectful distance and waiting with you rather than rushing between rumours. Days here are spent between the river and the surrounding plains, where the migration grazes in its tens of thousands. We pair the north with the central Serengeti, and often Ngorongoro, staying in camps chosen to keep you close to the river.

    When can I see a Mara River crossing?

    Crossings usually take place between July and October, when the migration is in the northern Serengeti and the Maasai Mara. The exact timing depends on rainfall and grazing, so it shifts a little each year. August and September are often considered the most reliable months, but crossings can happen at any point in the season, and herds may cross back and forth several times.

    Is a crossing guaranteed?

    No, and any honest operator will tell you the same. The herds decide when to cross, and sometimes they gather for hours and then walk away. What we can do is base you close to the river, give you enough time and work with guides who know how to read the herds. With two or three full days in the season, the chances are good.

    How do I get to the northern Serengeti?

    Most of our itineraries drive from the central Serengeti, which takes around two to three hours plus game viewing along the way. It is a scenic journey through changing landscapes. For travellers short on time, light-aircraft flights to airstrips in the north are an option, and we can combine flying and driving within one itinerary.

    Are the crossings crowded?

    Popular crossing points can attract a number of vehicles in peak season, and park rules govern how vehicles position themselves. A private safari gives you flexibility to choose quieter spots, move early and stay late. Our guides prioritise a calm approach that does not disturb the animals, because a well-placed vehicle at a respectful distance often has the best view anyway.

    How should I combine the north with other parks?

    A classic route begins in Tarangire, continues to the central Serengeti, heads north for two or three nights at the river, returns south and finishes in the Ngorongoro Crater. This gives you variety: elephants and baobabs, big cats, the crossings and a chance of rhino. Around ten to twelve days allows the journey without feeling rushed.

    Is the northern Serengeti suitable outside the migration season?

    Yes, for the right traveller. Without the herds the north is very quiet, and resident lions, elephants and plains game remain. It suits people who prefer solitude over spectacle. Some seasonal camps close, and the long rains in April and May can make roads slow, so we will advise honestly on whether it fits your dates.
